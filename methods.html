How the library was built

Loading the live counts…

This is an attempt to find every published case in which a serious psychiatric syndrome ended unexpectedly: the stroke that ended a craving, the infection that cleared a psychosis, the drug for something else that lifted a depression. Each case is appraised the same way and read with the boring explanations first. The aim is a library a clinician or researcher can trust enough to mine for mechanisms.

What counts as a case

  1. A serious psychiatric syndrome at diagnostic level, chronic (six months or more) or resistant to treatment (two or more adequate trials).
  2. It ended: complete remission, a fall of half or more on a rating scale, or a clearly described remission.
  3. The exposure wasn't aimed at it, or was aimed at it with no plausible expectation of benefit at the time: a stroke, surgery, a drug for something else, an infection, an accident, a life event.
  4. It was unexpected, judged by four tests: the exposure wasn't targeted at the syndrome; a typical clinician of the period wouldn't have predicted it; it wasn't a secondary syndrome clearing with its cause (delirium, a substance-induced state, treated encephalitis); and spontaneous remission isn't common at that chronicity.

Out: deliberate treatment, trials and cohorts (kept as context where useful), placebo responders, reviews without a new case, animal work. Cases first observed before 1970 are judged by the standards of their own period and shown as an antique section.

Where we looked

Search strings were tuned against 50 known cases before any screening began (42 of 50 found). Then:

  • Europe PMC, which covers MEDLINE and more: one broad query of about 43,000 records, loaded in year slices.
  • Embase and PsycInfo through Queensland Health's Clinical Knowledge Network: title, abstract, non-English, and "unexpected outcome" queries.
  • Citation chasing: the references and citing papers of every case, repeated from each new batch of cases until it stopped finding any.
  • Antique material: period vocabulary (melancholia, dementia praecox, morphinism) and the scanned back issues of old journals.
  • Free-copy sweeps (OpenAlex) for every case still waiting on its full text.

Where a list was too long to screen in full, it was ranked (first by keywords, later by a classifier trained on the cases already found) and screened from the top until a stopping rule was met: 300 consecutive irrelevant records, plus none found in a random sample of 300 from the unscreened tail.

From records to cases

How each case is appraised

  • Attribution score, out of 6: a documented chronic baseline (1), a standardised measure before and after (1), a change in a plausible window after the exposure (1), a reversal, that is symptoms returning when the exposure stopped and going again when it came back (2), and confounders addressed (1).
  • Reporting quality: the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist for case reports or case series.
  • Tier: A is strong evidence for that single case, B supporting, C logged but weak or carrying an integrity flag (a retraction, a predatory venue, an overclaim). Flagged cases are shown, never promoted.
  • A timeline for each patient, from onset through failed treatments, the exposure, the first change, any relapse and the last follow-up, so latency and durability can be compared across cases.
  • Our reading: seven boring explanations rated first (natural history, regression to the mean, misdiagnosis or a secondary syndrome, a co-intervention, non-specific suppression, expectancy, a reporting artefact), then a verdict, then ranked mechanisms, each with what drives the uncertainty and the test that would decide it. Readings are ours, not the papers', and stay drafts until reviewed.
  • Where it stands now: whether the finding was later tested (similar cases, pooled data, a randomised trial), became known, or was refuted.

Limitations

  • Publication bias is the point and the problem. Striking remissions get written up; failures of the same exposure mostly don't. The library shows what has happened, not how often.
  • A single case can't prove cause. Reversals and tight timing help; most cases have neither.
  • Some full texts were out of reach. Those cases are appraised from what could be read, and labelled.
  • One main screener. Claude screened and appraised; Brendan calibrated a blind sample and adjudicated disagreements.
  • Language. English sources first; non-English papers were included where an abstract or text could be read, but systematic non-English searching is still to come.
